The House can switch the feed off now, without waiting for Brussels

Brussels has known for two years and still cannot deliver a binding rule against addictive design. Kathmann's proposal lets our own parliament act, and it does not need permission.

3 November 2026

While Brussels lobbies and delays, the Dutch parliament can switch the feed off. On 2 November PvdA MP Barbara Kathmann proposed a law to do exactly that. Endless scrolling and luring notifications, the tricks that keep children glued and spending, would legally count as banned unfair commercial practices, oneerlijke handelspraktijken. A warning first, then a fine, and finally the platform itself goes dark. She wants unsafe platforms sent back to the drawing board. Her proposal reaches past social media into games, dating apps and chatbots. Millions of Dutch people say social media makes them unhappy, and the online world is always among the top five causes of young people's failing mental health. Nobody in The Hague needs to wait for permission. The House should adopt her proposal and make the cabinet defend it.

The Commission has known for two years. Its own investigators found in 2024 that autoplay, infinite scroll and highly personalised feeds push users into compulsive use. In February 2026 it issued preliminary findings against TikTok. In July 2026 it found Meta built Facebook and Instagram to be addictive, ignoring how much time minors spend on its apps at night, and demanded autoplay and infinite scroll be disabled by default, with a possible fine of up to 6 percent of global revenue. Preliminary findings. Still no binding rule. No final decision yet. Meanwhile its officials warn that national draft laws on minors risk fragmenting the single market, reducing legal certainty and raising compliance costs.

A warning against the very parliaments that pay its bills. And Kathmann is not alone: parliamentarians in Germany, France, Belgium, Luxembourg, Austria, Italy and Portugal are drafting the same law. In October 2026 the German government was examining its own rules against addictive design. Emmanuel Macron submitted a revised French proposal in September 2026, no longer banning named platforms but listing criteria such as addictive algorithms and around-the-clock notifications. The cabinet itself put addictive design and loot boxes on the EU agenda and signed the Jutland Declaration with 24 other states. Now it hesitates at home while a Dutch MP does the work.

We have heard its warnings about fragmentation before. And the French Constitutional Council struck down France's law in September 2026 as a disproportionate interference with freedom of opinion and communication. That objection answers the Commission, not us. In almost all other member states, consultations are still ongoing. A court ruling against one law does not ban legislating. It means we write the next law properly.

So the demand is plain. The House adopts Kathmann's proposal as it stands. The cabinet must defend it in Brussels instead of echoing the Commission's warnings about fragmentation. If the Commission wants one rule for all of Europe, it can take the drafts those parliamentarians have already written. Enforcement must in the end happen at EU level, she says, but a member state should not be afraid to lead the way. We pay for the Commission. Our parliament answers to us. It does not need to wait for the Digital Fairness Act, expected on 11 November 2026, with years of negotiation after. It should legislate now.
