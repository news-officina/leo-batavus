The Netherlands decides.

Opinion

We can switch off addictive apps ourselves. The Hague should.

Brussels has accused Meta of addicting users and fined nobody so far. Kathmann's law passes the judgement to a Dutch parliament, which is exactly where it belongs.

3 November 2026

Nobody voted for a Dutch ban on addictive online design, and nobody in Brussels can tell us what our children may be protected from. On 2 November, PvdA MP Barbara Kathmann introduced a Dutch law to ban addictive design: endless scrolling, autoplay, notifications built to drag people into payments. The European Commission has warned against national drafts like this, saying they fragment the single market. That warning is not a verdict on the law. It is Brussels holding on to a decision that belongs to the House of Representatives. Millions of Dutch people report unhappiness because of social media, and the online world sits in the top five of causes behind young people's deteriorating mental health.

Now look at what Brussels has actually delivered. In July 2026 it issued preliminary findings under the Digital Services Act, the EU rulebook for platforms, accusing Meta of building Facebook and Instagram to addict users. The Commission found autoplay and infinite scroll push users into compulsive use, and judged Meta's time management tools too easy to dismiss. A fine of up to 6 percent of global revenue is possible, roughly $12 billion on Meta's 2025 turnover of about $201 billion. Preliminary is the key word. TikTok received the same findings in February 2026. Meta contests them. TikTok contests them. This is Meta's third set of preliminary findings under the DSA. Not one final fine has been issued. The findings stay preliminary, and in the meantime the scrolling goes on.

Compare that with an Amsterdam court. Meta moved there, Kathmann says, when a judge enforced something: the Dutch case produced the only account in Europe free of algorithmic feeds on Instagram and Facebook. That is the answer to anyone who says only Brussels can reach the platforms. Kathmann says a court in our own capital made one of the largest companies on earth change course. Her proposal does it through existing consumer rules: first a warning, then a fine, and for a repeat offender, the platform is switched off. The tools exist. The Hague holds them.

The serious objection runs: platforms cross borders, and the Commission says diverging national drafts increase compliance costs and reduce legal certainty. In September 2026, the French Constitutional Council struck down France's own law to restrict social media for under-15s as a disproportionate interference with freedom of opinion and communication. We accept the point about patchwork. It argues for a Dutch law built on the consumer principles already agreed across the EU, not for waiting. The Brussels cases drag on, unfinished. Waiting for it is choosing it.

The Netherlands itself told Brussels, in its response on the expected Digital Fairness Act, that it wants a harmonised European solution to avoid fragmenting the single market. That is wrong, and the cabinet should say so plainly. The Act is not proposed until 11 November. Put the Kathmann proposal to a vote in the House, and demand the cabinet state whether it backs national rules or keeps waiting on Brussels. Kathmann is not alone: parliamentarians in Germany, France, Belgium, Luxembourg, Austria, Italy and Portugal want the same law, so it can no longer be lobbied away in Brussels. The House should pass it.
